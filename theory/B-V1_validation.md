# B-V1: Analytical vs Simulated η(d), P_L(d), η(f) and Mode Splitting

Generated by `python -m sim.compare_circuit`. Parameters from `sim/params.py`: $L_1=L_2=17.13$ µH,
$f_0=500$ kHz, $R_1=R_2=0.455$ Ω (C-T2 nominal; 0.40 Ω before 2026-10-25), $R_L=6.0$ Ω (C-C1), $V_s=5$ V peak, $R_s=0$;
$M(d)$ and $k(d)$ from `data/sim_M_k.csv` (A-S1). No measurements are involved (no-hardware roadmap).

"Simulation" is the phasor solver `sim/circuit.py` `solve()`, which solves the 2×2 mesh system
by Cramer's rule. "Analytical" is one of the closed forms derived in B-T1 and B-T2.

## 1. At $f_0$, versus spacing (B-T2 closed forms)

$$
\eta=\frac{\omega_0^2M^2R_L}{(R_2+R_L)\left[R_1(R_2+R_L)+\omega_0^2M^2\right]},\qquad
\eta_{max}=\frac{x}{(1+\sqrt{1+x})^2},
$$

$$
P_L=\frac{\tfrac12V_s^2\,\omega_0^2M^2R_L}{\left[(R_1+R_s)(R_2+R_L)+\omega_0^2M^2\right]^2},\qquad
|I_1|=\frac{V_s}{R_1+R_s+\omega_0^2M^2/(R_2+R_L)} .
$$

| Quantity (d = 0.5–15 cm) | Max relative error, solver vs closed form |
|---|---:|
| η(d), $R_L=6.0$ Ω | 4.9 × 10⁻¹⁶ |
| η_max(d), $R_L=R_{L,opt}(d)$ | 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ |
| P_L(d), $R_L=6.0$ Ω | 9.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ |
| \|I₁\|(d), $R_L=6.0$ Ω | 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ |

The errors are at machine precision. At $f_0$ both sides are the same algebra, so this
verifies the code, not the model. Figure: `figs/fig_eta_PL_vs_d_validation`.

## 2. Versus frequency (B-T1 Section 5, elimination route)

Off resonance the comparison uses a different route from the solver: eliminate $I_2$ to get
$Z_{in}=Z_1+\omega^2M^2/Z_2$, then $I_1=V_s/(Z_{in}+R_s)$, $|I_2|=\omega M|I_1|/|Z_2|$ and

$$
\eta(\omega)=\frac{\omega^2M^2R_L}{R_1|Z_2|^2+\omega^2M^2(R_2+R_L)} .
$$

Over 300–800 kHz (0.6–1.6 $f_0$, 40 001 points) at d = 1, 2, 5 and 10 cm, the largest relative
error is 8.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ for η(f) and 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ for P_L(f). The solver is therefore correct
at every frequency, including the split region. Figure: `figs/fig_PL_vs_f_validation`.

## 3. Mode splitting: solver peaks vs $f_0/\sqrt{1\pm k}$

The lossless coupled-mode frequencies (B-T1 Section 12, C-T1) are $f_l=f_0/\sqrt{1+k}$ and
$f_h=f_0/\sqrt{1-k}$, and $k=(f_h^2-f_l^2)/(f_h^2+f_l^2)$. The solver's $P_L(f)$ peaks are
compared with them for a lightly loaded secondary and for the fixed load
(`data/val_split_peaks.csv`):

| d | k | $R_L$ | Peaks, solver (kHz) | $f_0/\sqrt{1\pm k}$ (kHz) | k from peaks | Error in k |
|---:|---:|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 cm | 0.513 | 0.01 Ω | 406.50 / 716.50 | 406.48 / 716.53 | 0.513 | −0.012 % |
| 1 cm | 0.513 | 6.0 Ω | 407.93 / 713.99 |  | 0.508 | −1.0 % |
| 2 cm | 0.321 | 0.01 Ω | 435.04 / 606.79 | 435.02 / 606.82 | 0.321 | −0.026 % |
| 2 cm | 0.321 | 6.0 Ω | 437.50 / 603.33 |  | 0.311 | −3.2 % |
| 5 cm | 0.111 | 0.01 Ω | 474.40 / 530.26 | 474.33 / 530.35 | 0.111 | −0.28 % |
| 5 cm | 0.111 | 6.0 Ω | 483.50 / 519.64 |  | 0.072 | **−35.3 %** |
| 10 cm | 0.030 | 0.01 Ω | 493.00 / 507.32 | 492.69 / 507.64 | 0.029 | −4.2 % |
| 10 cm | 0.030 | 6.0 Ω | single peak at 500.0 | | – | – |

With a lightly loaded secondary the formula holds to 0.3 % in k down to 5 cm. At 10 cm the coil
resistance alone already pulls the peaks in by 4 %, because $kQ\approx3.5$ is no longer large.
The 6 Ω load damps the secondary ($Q_{2,L}=8.3$) and pulls the peaks together. At 5 cm the formula
then reads k 35 % low, and at 10 cm ($k<k_{split}=0.085$, C-C1) there is only one peak. So k must
be read from the split peaks with the secondary shorted or lightly loaded (A-T3 item 6).
Figure: `figs/fig_split_vs_k_validation`.

## 4. Sensitivity to the coil resistance

C-T2 gives $R_1=R_2=0.38$–$0.48$ Ω (`params.R_range`). Efficiency over that range
(`data/val_eta_Rband.csv`, `figs/fig_eta_vs_d_Rband`):

| d | η, $R_L=6.0$ Ω (R = 0.38 / 0.455 / 0.48 Ω) | η_max (R = 0.38 / 0.455 / 0.48 Ω) |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 cm | 93.7 / 92.6 / 92.2 % | 97.3 / 96.8 / 96.6 % |
| 3 cm | 92.4 / 91.0 / 90.5 % | 93.7 / 92.5 / 92.1 % |
| 5 cm | 88.1 / 85.9 / 85.2 % | 88.1 / 85.9 / 85.2 % |
| 8 cm | 69.1 / 64.7 / 63.3 % | 74.7 / 70.5 / 69.2 % |
| 10 cm | 48.6 / 43.5 / 42.0 % | 62.6 / 57.2 / 55.5 % |

Up to 5 cm the uncertainty in R moves η by at most +2.2 / −0.7 points; at 10 cm by up to +5.4 / −1.7 points.
The 6.0 Ω load stays the efficiency optimum at 5 cm over the whole range ($R_{L,opt}$ = 6.00–6.01 Ω),
because for $x\gg1$, $R_{L,opt}=R_2\sqrt{1+x}\to\omega_0M$, which does not depend on R.

## 5. Summary

1. The solver matches the B-T2 closed forms at $f_0$ to machine precision (< 10⁻¹⁵), for η, η_max, P_L and $I_1$.
2. It matches the independent B-T1 elimination route at every frequency from 300 to 800 kHz (< 10⁻¹⁴).
3. With a lightly loaded secondary, the split peaks reproduce $f_0/\sqrt{1\pm k}$, and k is recovered to 0.3 % for d ≤ 5 cm.
4. With the 6 Ω load the peaks are pulled together (k reads 35 % low at 5 cm, single peak at 10 cm), so the formula needs $R_L\to0$.
5. Over C−T2's R range of 0.38–0.48 Ω, η_max changes by at most +2.2 / −0.7 points up to 5 cm and by up to +5.4 / −1.7 points at 10 cm.
6. LTspice (`sim/wpt.asc`) and the solver agree to better than 10⁻⁹ % at k = 0.321, 0.111 and 0.030 (target < 1 %), and both give I₂ lagging I₁ by 90° at f₀.

## 6. LTspice cross-check (B-V1 part 2)

Sections 1–3 compare the solver with algebra derived from the same circuit equations. The
independent check is the LTspice schematic `sim/wpt.asc` (C-S1), run in LTspice 26.0.1 (under Wine)
by `python -m sim.compare_ltspice --run`:

* LTspice turns `wpt.asc` into a netlist. The wiring checks out: Vs → C1 → R1 → L1 → ground in the
  primary, L2 → C2 → R2 → R_L → ground in the secondary, K1 couples L1 and L2. The dots are at the
  top terminals (N002, N003), so I(L1) and I(L2) both enter the dots, as in B-T1.
* `.options plotwinsize=0 numdgt=15` is added so LTspice stores uncompressed double-precision data.
  The `.ac` sweep (3001 points, 350–650 kHz) is run for the three k values of the `.step` list.
  The complex currents are read from the binary `.raw` file and saved in `data/ltspice_ac.csv`.
* The solver uses the same circuit: C = 5.914815 nF as typed in `wpt.asc` (params: 5.914839 nF),
  k from the `.step` list, and L, R, R_L, V_s from `params.py`.

Results (`data/val_ltspice_metrics.csv`, `figs/fig_PL_eta_vs_f_ltspice`):

| d | k | $P_L$ peak frequency, LTspice / solver (kHz) | $P_L$ peak, LTspice / solver (W) | η at $f_0$, LTspice / solver | ∠($I_2/I_1$) at $f_0$ | Largest difference over the sweep |
|---:|---:|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| 2 cm | 0.3211 | 437.498 / 437.498 | 1.63511 / 1.63511 | 92.046 / 92.046 % | −89.998° | 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ % |
| 5 cm | 0.1112 | 483.500 / 483.500 | 2.14774 / 2.14774 | 85.905 / 85.905 % | −89.998° | 4 × 10⁻¹⁰ % |

LTspice and the solver agree to better than 10⁻⁹ % in |I₁|, |I₂|, P_L and η at every frequency.
The project target is < 1 %. Such close agreement is expected, because an `.ac` analysis of a
linear circuit solves the same linear equations exactly. The value of the check is that an
independently drawn schematic, with its own wiring, coupling element and dot placement, gives
the same answer as the Python model. The phase of $I_2/I_1$ at 500 kHz is −89.998° rather than
−90°, in both, because the typed C (5.914815 nF) puts the resonance 1 Hz above 500 kHz.

Notes for C-S1:

  is run with the netlist's own k.
